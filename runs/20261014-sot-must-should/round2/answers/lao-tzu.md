## How I decided

1. **My #2** — 知足不辱，知止不殆，可以長久 — set the size before the content. RFC 2119 §6 says MUST is used with care and sparingly, so the first question was how many stops this design can afford, not what any one id means.
2. **My #1** — 損之又損，以至於無為 — is the order of work: I read every clause as MUST and took the MUSTs away one at a time until only the unrecoverable ones were left.
3. **The test, my reading of the application** (using my #16 知人者智，自知者明 and my #12 將欲廢之，必固興之): name the case that breaks; if a later act can restore the record — a superseding entry, or git as an ordinary reader uses it — the loss is recoverable and the level is SHOULD.
4. **My #4** — 為者敗之，執者失之; 天下神器，不可為也 — keeps the second question apart from the first: *does something stop the work today?* A `Fails if` clause is the way a requirement is checked, not its weight. The tool's existing checks (empty or duplicate id, kind/prefix mismatch in an active entry, missing `**Why:**`, empty claim, status outside the three, index mismatch, index over its size limits) make a clause **free**, not important; free clauses (W1.1's uniqueness, W1.3's `**Why:**`, W4.1's `status`, K6.1's bound) read MUST so that a writer meeting the tool's refusal does not take it for a bug and patch the tool. Clauses the tool is silent on stay at SHOULD.
5. **My #13** — 善有果而已; 其事好還 — did the demotions the round-2 fact forced. Round 1 I made the judged MUSTs conditional on a reviewer existing; that condition is now settled, so judged requirements drop unless the loss is unrecoverable. That moved W2.2 and Rd2.1 down and split W4.1, W6.1 and Rd2.3.
6. **My #12** — 將欲廢之，必固興之 — named the case for each MUST I kept: a merge dropping one side (W6.3); an id or prefix changed under links that name it (W1.1); an entry deleted for age (K4.2); two claims sharing one file (W1.3, W6.1); another agent's state or a person's memory in the repo (W3.3, W3.5); a session's state that existed only in its context (W5.1).
7. **My #19** — 以深為根，以約為紀; 苟免於咎 — sized each deviation: an overridable default with the reason written down is the right weight for a shape, an order, or a place to put things.
8. **My #17** — 故有之以為利，無之以為用; "the words do not say to remove anything; that would be an application" — put the level on the operative clause, not the prose around it. W2.1's definitions, W4.1's permission, Rd2.1's loading mechanisms and K6.1's generation method are the vessel's shape, not rules in it.
9. **My #16** caught me in W4.2: I had claimed the in-file log was the only record, while W4.3 of the same document says non-entries rely on git history. 自知者明 — so the correction belongs in the row, not in a footnote.
10. **My #10** — 今舍慈且勇，舍儉且廣，舍後且先，死矣 — names what the whole answer is built on: nothing private in a shared repo (W3.3, W3.5), nothing lost (K4, W6.3, W5.1), and memory not made the centre of the design — no owner, no approval, no number of my own. Give up one of the three while keeping the other two and the loss starts quietly.
11. **My #8** — 知不知上，不知知病 — is the label on all of it: n=0, nothing measured. My reading of the tool-enforced clauses is a reading; on Rd3.1's figures I have nothing at all.
12. **My #6 and #7** — 為之於未有，治之於未亂 with 慎終如始，則無敗事 — set when to look again: levels are cheapest to fix at n=0, and the failure of a document like this arrives at the end, in the rows nobody revisits.

## What I need to know

1. **Who is the reviewer for a judged check, and at what named moment?** If one is assigned, these return to MUST in this order: **W2.2's labelling first** (Rd5.5 says in the document's own words that checking against the code cannot catch a wrong fact about the outside world, and a guess is exactly that), then **W3.4**, then **Rd2.1**, then **Rd4.5** and **Rd2.3's** four context items.
2. **Is the designed fix — id form and conflict markers — landing, and does it cover both?** If it does, W1.1's id form and K6.3's markers become free instead of reader-enforced; my levels do not move, only the enforcement does.
3. **W3.2 against K4.1/K4.3:** if EJ settles it so a credential inside a claim can never be removed or rewritten, then a pre-commit check is the only remedy that exists — and with no secret scanner, W3.2 is currently unenforceable. My levels do not change; the truth of W3.2 does.
4. **K4.3 against W6.3:** if K4.3's list of what an entry may gain is exhaustive, "both links" cannot be satisfied and W6.3's MUST narrows to both `changed:` lines and both claims. The level stays MUST; the clause narrows.
5. **What is the measured strictest start-file limit among the agents that will read this project, and is that the number `memory_index.py` uses?** If the tool's limit is not the measured one, Rd3.1's MUST is a gate on the wrong figure, and the figure is what needs fixing.
6. **Does the `R-` prefix removal land before the first lint?** If a lint ships against the unmerged prefix set, W1.1's five prefixes and the tool's enum disagree, and the tool will refuse correct entries.

## Costs and risks

- **Demoting the judged rows leaves six rules that nothing stops** — W2.2's labelling, W3.4, Rd2.1, Rd2.3's four context items and Rd4.5. An ignored MUST at least leaves a hole someone can find; I have traded that for honesty about who is actually checking (my #13: things tend to come back).
- **The whole Write section now rests on readers.** With no lint over `entries/` and no reviewer, W1.1 through W5.3 have no gate except the tool's seven checks. That is the price of refusing to call unassigned judgement a gate (my #2).
- **My remaining MUSTs are also unenforced** — W1.1's never-change, W1.3's one claim, W3.3, W3.5, W5.1's handoff, W6.1, W6.3, K4.2. One git-side check — deletions and renames by `--diff-filter`, plus resolving every id named in `superseded_by` and `depends_on` — would cover most of them at once. I offer that as guidance, not as a new MUST: it is one method, not eight (my #4).
- **W1.3's `**Why:**` promotion stands only while K4.3's list is exhaustive.** If EJ widens it, a missing reason becomes repairable and the clause returns to SHOULD.
- **K4.2 and K6.1's one-line-per-active-entry push the index toward Rd3.1's ceiling**, and the only exit the design offers is retire-with-link, which costs a writer per old entry (my #2: know where to stop).
- **Rd5.3 concentrates a decision in one person** — the same concentration W4.1 removes elsewhere (my #9: put yourself behind).
- **Nothing here is measured** (my #8). If the first twenty commits break a MUST I kept, that row was decoration, and I say so in advance rather than after.

## How we would know I was right

- **The first twenty commits.** Count every violation of a MUST clause I kept. Zero is the prediction. One violation of W1.3's one-claim rule, W6.1, K4.2 or W6.3 means my test was wrong and those rows belong at SHOULD — the level was decoration (my #14: 少則得, little is gained).
- **The handoff count (Rd6.1).** Questions asked that a decision entry or a `state.md` already answers. One or more means Rd2.1 at SHOULD was too low, and my promotion order in "What I need to know" was right.
- **The guess count (W2.2).** Over the first twenty entries, count entries whose `kind` is `fact` or `decision` and whose body reads as a guess. I expect at least one; if none appears, the demotion was harmless, and if several do, it was wrong.
- **The load count (Rd2.3).** Sessions that loaded more than the index and what they needed. If most sessions blow past the list at SHOULD, the list needs a machine check, not a stronger word.
- **One sentence to watch for:** an agent refusing a change because "the memory rules require it". That is a method being gripped (my #4), and it means a level is wrong.
- **Re-read at** twenty entries, or the day a lint over `entries/` or a reviewer exists, whichever comes first, with the demoted rows decided then (my #7: 慎終如始).
